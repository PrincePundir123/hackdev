03 / COMPLETE PROBLEM STATEMENT

The gap between noticing and helping

Older adults living independently may need occasional support with everyday routines or unexpected situations, while trusted relatives may be geographically distant, busy, or unsure who is responsible. The problem is the absence of a simple, privacy-respecting process that turns a request or concerning event into timely, acknowledged human follow-through.

In a common situation, an older adult has a phone, a few family contacts, and a routine that changes from day to day. A relative may call to check in, but a missed call has several possible explanations. The person may be resting, their phone may be charging, or they may need help. Repeated calls can become intrusive, while silence can produce uncertainty. Neither silence nor a missing sensor reading proves an emergency.

When a message is sent to several relatives, everyone may assume someone else will respond. One person may read the alert but be unable to help. Another may call without informing the group. A system can display a successful notification while the practical question—“Who is handling this?”—remains unanswered. This is a coordination failure as much as a sensing problem.

Five connected barriers

  • Interaction burden: small controls, complicated navigation, and repeated data entry can make routine help harder to request.
  • Unclear ownership: a broadcast message does not assign responsibility, record a decline, or confirm follow-through.
  • Loss of context: fixed alarms cannot always express requests tied to a person’s routine; fragmented messages lose relevant details.
  • Privacy tradeoffs: a person may want a neighbour to help without exposing medication notes or private conversations.
  • Uncertain system state: missing connectivity, stale events, and failed deliveries can look like normal inactivity unless explicitly shown.

WHO projects that one in six people globally will be aged 60 or older by 2030. This establishes the scale of ageing, but it does not prove demand for this particular application. User interviews and usability testing are still needed. [6]

HACKATHON PROBLEM STATEMENT

How might we help an older adult request and coordinate everyday support through a simple interface, while ensuring that only approved information reaches the right people and that every request has a visible response status?

Scope: daily support, check-ins, and coordination. The prototype does not diagnose illness, establish whether a fall occurred, verify medication ingestion, or provide a guaranteed emergency response.

05 / PROPOSED SOLUTION & DIFFERENTIATION

Saathi Care AI: a care circle that follows through

Saathi Care AI is a proposed responsive web app with two role-specific experiences: a simple personal companion and a focused caregiver workspace. The backend converts a confirmed request into a reminder or care incident, applies current permissions, and tracks the human response until an explicit outcome is recorded.

The product has three connected loops

  • Trust loop: preview what each contact may see, update permissions, and inspect a record of decisions and disclosures.

08 / USER JOURNEYS

Three stories that define the build

Journey A — a reminder linked to a routine

Meera types or says, “Remind me to water the plants twenty minutes after my morning check-in.” The assistant proposes a reminder with trigger = morning check-in and offset = 20 minutes. The screen reads back the interpretation and asks for confirmation. After saving, Meera taps “I am okay” at 8:10 a.m.; the backend records that event and calculates a due time of 8:30 a.m. The reminder appears once and can be marked done or postponed.

Important branches: If no check-in occurs, the reminder remains waiting for its trigger. If Meera wants a fallback clock time, that preference must be explicitly configured. Repeated taps do not create duplicate deliveries. A late event should be explained before an overdue reminder is delivered. Morning check-in is a self-report event, not proof that a wearable detected waking.

Journey B — a support request reaches an owner

Meera presses “Request help” and confirms a simple message. The backend creates an incident and contacts Arjun. His screen offers “I will respond” and “I cannot respond.” If he declines or the configured wait expires, the workflow checks who is currently authorized and available, then contacts Leela. Leela accepts. The incident shows her as the responder while remaining open until she records an outcome.

Important branches: A late acceptance from Arjun must not create two owners. An expired invitation cannot acknowledge a new incident. If Leela accepts but does not follow up, the system records an overdue follow-up and applies the household’s configured backup process. If nobody accepts, it displays “No responder confirmed,” not “Help is on the way.”

Journey C — privacy changes during a request

Meera permits Leela to receive basic support requests but not private care notes. A preview shows the exact allowed view. During an incident, Meera revokes Leela’s access. The backend checks the latest policy before the next disclosure or fetch; future access is blocked and any pending notification job is re-evaluated. Information already delivered cannot be recalled, so the interface explains that limit.

REHEARSAL MODE

The same three journeys run in drill mode with fictional data, conspicuous “TEST” labels, shortened timers, and test recipients. A drill validates the software path and human response process, not real-world emergency readiness.

These journeys define the first backlog and the demo. Every extra feature should explain which step it improves.

The AI component should make interaction easier. It interprets a person’s words into a small set of supported intents and can produce concise explanations from authorized structured facts. It should not independently decide that somebody is safe, invent medical advice, approve a disclosure, or suppress a direct request for help.

10 / CONSENT & INFORMATION SHARING

Make permissions understandable and enforceable

Consent is a product interaction and a server-side rule. A contact may be allowed to receive a minimal support request while being unable to read private notes. Separate the permission to contact someone from permission to disclose each data category. An older adult should be able to understand and change both.

Information / actionArjun: primaryLeela: backup

This is an example configuration, not a default entitlement for family members. At setup, show contact-specific choices in plain language. An invitation should tell the recipient what participation involves and let them decline. The initial prototype assumes the older adult can consent; legal-representative or guardianship workflows are outside its scope.

The authorization sequence

Authenticate → identify household and resource → load current permission → check action and fields → construct the smallest allowed response → record the decision. Apply the same sequence to API reads, queued notifications, AI context, exports, and acknowledgment actions. Hiding a field in the interface is insufficient if the API still returns it.

Use a single policy module with deny-by-default behavior. A Cedar-based policy layer is a possible implementation choice consistent with the supplied PDF; a smaller deterministic policy module is acceptable if the scope and tests are explicit. Store policy version and contact identity with each decision. Avoid sending broad health details in lock-screen notifications.

Revocation and fallback

When permission changes, invalidate future access and re-evaluate queued messages. Keep a minimal audit entry of the change. Do not silently override a denial because an event is labelled urgent. Any exceptional sharing rule would require deliberate prior design and appropriate review; the MVP should use only explicitly authorized contacts and fields.

THE TRUST FEATURE

Preview example: “Leela can see that you asked for a check-in and when you asked. She cannot see your private notes.” The preview should be generated by the same permission logic used for real delivery.

A notification is not a completed response

Persist an incident state machine so the application can explain exactly what it knows. Keep provider delivery status, caregiver acknowledgment, and actual resolution separate. A successful HTTP request to a notification service only proves that a request was accepted at that layer.

Race conditions and retries

Give each source event a unique ID and every notification attempt a deduplication key. Create at most one active incident for a repeated event. Acknowledge by an atomic conditional update: the first valid acceptance wins, and later attempts receive the current owner state. An expired or revoked invitation cannot claim ownership. If a timeout and an acceptance arrive together, resolve them against the same persisted version.

An acceptance can cancel future invitations, but it cannot retract a message already received. Tell a second responder that someone else has accepted. On restart, recover pending deadlines from storage. Retry transient failures with a bounded policy; after repeated failure, expose a visible failure state instead of endlessly claiming that contact is in progress.

Demo timing versus real timing

Use a 10-second primary-contact wait and a short follow-up timer only in drill mode. Display “Accelerated test timing.” These values exist to make the demo readable; they are not recommended emergency response thresholds. Real deployment would require context-specific design with users and qualified care partners.

Accessibility choices to implement

  • Use large touch controls, readable type, strong contrast, clear focus states, and text labels alongside icons.
  • Support keyboard navigation and screen-reader labels for primary controls. Announce significant status changes without repeatedly interrupting the user.
  • Offer a text path alongside voice. Show a transcript for correction and use push-to-talk with a visible recording indicator.

20 / REUSABLE PROJECT DESCRIPTION

Submission draft: adapt after the build

Project title: Saathi Care AI — Consent-Aware Elder-Care Coordination
Tagline: Simple requests. Clear permissions. A caregiver who confirms.

Problem we are addressing

Older adults living independently may need help with daily routines or unexpected situations, while their trusted family members live elsewhere or have limited availability. Phone calls, reminders, and group messages do not always provide a clear record of who is responding. At the same time, sharing every personal detail with every contact can undermine the older person’s privacy and independence. We want to make support easier to request, easier to coordinate, and easier to control.

What we propose to build

Saathi Care AI is an AI-assisted companion and care-circle coordination app. An older adult can create a reminder in everyday language, complete a simple check-in, or request help through a clear interface. The assistant turns supported language into a structured proposal that the user reviews before it is saved. Trusted contacts use a separate workspace to accept or decline support requests and record follow-up.

When a request is confirmed, the application checks which contacts are authorized and what information they may receive. It then invites the primary contact and waits for an explicit response. If that person cannot respond or the configured wait expires, the system can invite an approved backup. Acceptance assigns a named responder; resolution requires a separate outcome. A visible timeline explains every step.

What makes this implementation useful

Our proposed emphasis is understandable privacy and accountable follow-through. A contact-specific preview shows what information will be shared. A care-circle drill lets participants rehearse the workflow with synthetic events and shortened timers. Server-side permission checks, persistent incident state, and duplicate-event handling make the demonstration more than a conversational interface.

Inspiration and boundaries

This concept is inspired by the published Suraksha elder-care case study by Team StarBugs. The intended submission is a separate implementation developed for this event. It focuses on care coordination and user control. Simulated wearable inputs will be labelled, and the prototype will not claim medical diagnosis, validated fall detection, or guaranteed emergency response.

21 / ROADMAP & REAL-WORLD BOUNDARIES

What comes after a credible prototype

The hackathon should establish that the interaction and coordination mechanism works on a controlled setup. It cannot establish readiness for unsupervised elder care. Future work should reduce uncertainty in stages rather than adding more features before the basic process is trusted.

StageNext workEvidence needed
After the eventSupervised usability sessions, clearer consent, language improvementsParticipants understand actions and permissions; observed issues are documented.

Care-partner reviewDefine escalation expectations, accessibility needs, safeguarding boundariesAgreed responsibilities, qualified review, realistic operating procedures.
Deployment preparationSecurity assessment, data retention, support model, regional requirementsDocumented controls, incident handling, and appropriate specialist review.

Keep the older adult in control

Caregiver convenience should not silently become surveillance. Avoid continuous recording by default. Limit retained data and let the person inspect sharing decisions. A contact’s request for more information is not permission to provide it. If the person cannot provide informed consent, that requires a separately designed and appropriately reviewed support arrangement rather than an improvised family override.

Recognize the limits of observation

A missed check-in may reflect routine variation. A sensor reading may be noisy or delayed. A successful notification does not prove that it was read, and an acceptance does not prove that help arrived. The application should describe the evidence it has, preserve uncertainty, and support human judgment. Any future clinical claims would need suitable validation beyond this software demonstration.

Recommended final project commitment

Commit to a small care-coordination system with a voice or text front door, confirmed reminders, a permission-aware care circle, persistent acknowledgment and follow-up, and a clearly labelled drill. Keep wearable sensing as an optional input adapter. This is specific enough to build, substantial enough to demonstrate engineering, and coherent enough to explain in a short presentation.

FINAL PRODUCT DIRECTION

Your project’s strongest promise is: “We make it clear what was requested, what was shared, and who agreed to respond.” Let the working demo prove that promise.
